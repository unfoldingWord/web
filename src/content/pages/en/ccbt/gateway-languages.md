    body: |-
      A majority language is a Gateway Language only when the church needs it as a Language of Wider Communication. Churches bilingual in a gateway language then translate into their own languages. The strategy answers two obstacles: resources available only in English, and licenses that restrict who may use, translate, adapt or redistribute them.

      > **The Gateway Languages Strategy:** equip the global church with the content, tools and training needed for Bible translation and for creating theologically sound biblical resources, by providing them under open licenses and collaborating with church networks to translate them into the ~50 Languages of Wider Communication that cover the global church through multilingualism.
      >
      > — Tim Jore, *The Gateway Languages Strategy* (2017), executive summary

      [Gateway languages (GLs)](/ccbt/glossary/#gateway-languages) are the smallest number of Languages of Wider Communication that covers 100% of the languages spoken collectively by the people groups of the world through patterns of multilingualism. In practice the strategy names about 50 such languages. A majority language is a Gateway Language only when the church needs it as a Language of Wider Communication.

      [Other languages (OLs)](/ccbt/glossary/#other-languages): in the Gateway Languages strategy, all languages that are not considered gateway languages. Not a synonym for “minority languages.”

  - type: prose
    title: Why it matters for the church
    background: mist
    body: |-
      In 2001 a people group in Papua New Guinea decided they were not going to wait any longer for a missionary to translate the Bible into their language. They had asked for help more than ten years before, but no help had come. The church in that people group began translating the Bible into their own language.

      They met two obstacles:

      - **Language.** The training, exegetical resources and tools needed to translate with excellence were available only in English. Some of their leaders spoke some English, but they were not bilingual enough to use English-only resources with confidence.
      - **Legal.** Even if they could understand the resources, they could not legally use many of them. The licenses applied only to translators who were members of certain Western missionary organizations.

      Churches that are ready to translate still meet those same [two obstacles](/ccbt/glossary/#two-obstacles): the training, exegetical resources and tools needed to translate with excellence are available only in English, and licenses on those resources restrict who may use, translate, adapt and redistribute them.

      The remaining need includes more than languages with no Scripture: revisions, unfinished projects, other scripts and cultural forms, dialects, audio and complete Bibles rather than New Testaments only. Across formats, the remaining need “could easily be in excess of 10,000” (*The Gateway Languages Strategy*, 2017).

  - type: prose
    title: How it works
    body: |-
      In most places, multilingualism is ordinary: some members of a church understand a Language of Wider Communication. If the church has unrestricted content, tools and training in that language, it can use them and then translate into its own.

      A majority language is a [gateway language](/ccbt/glossary/#gateway-languages) only when the church needs it as a Language of Wider Communication to cover other languages through multilingualism, even if it has many speakers and high prestige. If the church can translate from an existing Gateway Language, it does not need another.

      - title: Collaborate
        body: Collaborate with church networks to translate those resources into the Gateway Languages.
      - title: Translate
        body: >-
          The church that is bilingual in a Gateway Language uses the resources and translates from the GL into
          its own other language.

  - type: prose
    title: Building a strong foundation
    body: |-
      The first stage takes time and capital, because creating content, building tools and translating them into about fifty Gateway Languages is slow, expensive work. The aim is forming capacity in the church so the church can translate.

      Open licenses and Gateway Language resources give the church what it needs to translate with excellence and to check what it produces, but a draft still needs checking, and the strategy requires training in translation principles and mature leadership in the church.

      If no church yet exists, evangelism and church planting come first, and an outside-in approach may still be fitting since God has used that work. English-only, restricted resources cannot equip the church that is already translating. [Church-Centric Bible Translation](/ccbt/) names the larger aim: establishing the church, with translation as a means.
