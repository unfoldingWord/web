Church-Centric Bible Translation (CCBT) is a paradigm of Bible translation focused on the establishing and strengthening of a lingual church using a translation process that is led by leaders of the church and done by believers translating into their own language, as an integral part of their theological and spiritual formation. The church-centric paradigm has as its focus the establishing of the church, with Bible translation as a means to that end.

## Definition

> Church-Centric Bible Translation (CCBT): a paradigm of Bible translation focused on the establishing and strengthening of a lingual church using a translation process that is led by leaders of the church and done by believers translating into their own language, as an integral part of their theological and spiritual formation. The church-centric paradigm has as its focus the establishing of the church, with Bible translation as a means to that end.
>
> Tim Jore, From Unreached to Established (2018), Glossary.

A [lingual church](/ccbt/glossary/#lingual-church) is a portion of the [global church](/ccbt/glossary/#global-church) that is linguistically homogeneous, speaking either the same language or variants that have a sufficient degree of mutual intelligibility, and including all traditions that are faithful expressions of sound doctrine (Eph. 4:1–6; 1 Tim. 6:3; Tit. 2:1). An [established church](/ccbt/glossary/#established-church) is a church that is firmly rooted and grounded in sound doctrine (orthodoxy; cf. Eph. 4:11–16) and is characterized by faithful living (orthopraxy). The term is not synonymous with a planted church, and it is not the classic three-self rubric (self-supporting, self-propagating, self-governing), which addresses independence, not doctrinal integrity.

Believers translate into their own language as part of formation. They check the work for excellence, and the church that speaks the language is the one that must be able to affirm the result.

## CCBT, church-based, and Bible-centric

Church-based is broader than church-centric, and a Bible-centric project can still be church-based. CCBT is a subset of Church-Based Bible Translation (CBBT): the church does the work, and the aim is [establishing the church](/ccbt/establishing-the-church/).

|  | CCBT | CBBT | BCBT |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What it names | Church-Centric Bible Translation | Church-Based Bible Translation | Bible-Centric Bible Translation |
| Who leads | Leaders of the church; believers translating into their own language | The church itself does the translation work | A translation agency, using an academic and linguistic process |
| What is central | Establishing the church; translation is a means | That the church does the work (aim may vary) | Production of a quality Bible translation, with life transformation as the intended outcome |
| Relation | A subset of CBBT | Broader category; includes CCBT and some Bible-centric work | Can still be church-based. Church-centric only when the aim is establishing the church |

The wider church-based movement gathers at [churchbased.bible](https://churchbased.bible/), and this hub defines the church-centric paradigm.

## Establishing the church is the aim

Church-centric means to evaluate everything from the viewpoint of the needs of establishing and equipping the church (Acts 14:21–23; 15:40–41; Eph. 4:11–16). The ultimate objective of missions is the establishing of the church in each people group in sound doctrine, with mature leadership and the ability to understand the Word of God and defend sound doctrine for the glory of God.

Jesus sent the church to make disciples of all nations, baptizing them and teaching them to obey all that he commanded (Matt. 28:16–20). Paul and Barnabas returned after first preaching, strengthening the disciples, encouraging them to continue in the faith, and appointing elders in each church (Acts 14:21–23). Christ gave apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors and teachers “to equip the saints for works of ministry and to build up the body of Christ” (Eph. 4:11–12, BSB). God’s purpose is that now, through the church, the manifold wisdom of God should be made known (Eph. 3:10).

At the wedding feast of the Lamb, the church remains, purchased from every tribe and tongue (Rev. 5:9–10), while Bible agencies do not, and after a translation is finished, the church has to understand the Word, teach sound doctrine, and keep the translation trustworthy over time.

A Bible-centric paradigm has as its focus the production of a Bible translation, and that work has served the church well in settings where it fits. Delivering a product, even a good one, does not by itself [establish the church](/ccbt/establishing-the-church/). The church may leave unused a translation it did not form around, cannot check, or cannot revise. Bible-centric quality assurance also faces a personnel bottleneck as demand grows. The church-centric aim is building capacity in the lingual church, even when that takes longer than delivering a finished translation.

## Church-centric and Bible-centric sequences

The two paradigms follow different sequences, each with a different priority, and the sequence shapes what the church is left with when the work is done.

|  | Sequence | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bible-centric | Produce → Publish → Engage | A quality Bible, as fast as possible |
| Church-centric | Engage → Produce → Publish | Forming the lingual church in theological capacity and translation experience so the church can produce a [trustworthy and trusted](/ccbt/trustworthy-and-trusted/) translation |

In the church-centric sequence, leaders of the church engage first: they study, receive training, and take responsibility. The church produces Scripture, notes, training, and other biblical content as that capacity grows. Those leaders then publish, because the church that produced the text is the church that will use it, check it, and revise it. If translators produce a draft faster than the church can affirm it, the church is left with a text it cannot stand by. [Equipping servants](/ccbt/equipping-servants/), translocal workers who strengthen churches without replacing local elders, help with that first engagement and leave the translation with the church.

## Content, tools, and training

The church translates, checks, and owns the work, and unfoldingWord makes open-licensed biblical content, tools, and training available so church networks can do that work in their own languages as part of establishing the church.

Church networks use Gateway Language resources (including unfoldingWord Literal Text and Simplified Text, translationNotes, and unfoldingWord Open Bible Stories), training in translationAcademy, and checking tools such as translationCore. The [Gateway Languages Strategy](/ccbt/gateway-languages/) addresses two obstacles the church meets: the training, exegetical resources, and tools needed to translate with excellence are available only in English, and licenses on those resources restrict who may use, translate, adapt, and redistribute them. The church retains the freedom to translate and to build further resources through [open-licensed biblical content](/ccbt/open-licensed-biblical-content/). Church networks can follow the practical sequence in [how churches translate](/ccbt/how-churches-translate/).

## First the church, then translation

Where there is no church, the biblical pattern is to preach the gospel, make disciples, and appoint elders (Acts 14:21–23). Bible translation is a co-requisite of the church’s formation rather than a prerequisite to planting. In unreached settings, evangelists and church planters begin with understandable gospel proclamation, including Bible stories, sometimes in a language of wider communication. Once a church is planted, translation proceeds at a pace the leaders can understand and teach.
